# Exact endgames, not bigger networks, lift 21

The engine can realistically climb from about 8,840 to somewhere between **9,050 and 9,300** points per game, and a 10,000 average is unlikely: on the evidence available it probably sits above what even perfect play can average. The surest gain is one the project has already measured, the complete solve of the last 12 cards (**+108 ± 38**), which only needs to be made fast enough for a phone. The next gains come from rebuilding what the value network sees and what it is trained on, not from making it larger or searching deeper, both of which the project has already tested and found flat. No published solver, strategy analysis or open-source bot exists for this family of games, so there is no outside engine or expert knowledge to borrow; progress has to come from general methods proven in 2048, backgammon, chess and Go. The heavyweight training frameworks (MuZero-style) are a poor fit and should be skipped. Every expected gain below except the +108 is my estimate by analogy and must be confirmed on the project's own test decks; the published results come from other games and do not transfer as numbers.

## The plan in priority order, with honest expected gains

**Step 1 is a better measuring stick, and it comes first because every later step depends on it.** The project's recent experiments carry error bars of ±27 to ±97 points, which is larger than most of the gains still on offer. The order of the remaining deck does not depend on how the engine plays, so two engine versions can be run on exactly the same shuffled decks and compared game by game; the comparison is then far less noisy than two independent runs. This "same scenarios for every policy" idea is standard in the research literature ([Ng & Jordan 2000](https://arxiv.org/abs/1301.3878)), and the 2048 papers show what happens without it: headline results from 100 games carry uncertainty of roughly ±40,000 on scores near 600,000 ([Guei 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.11087)). This step adds no points itself. It makes a 15-to-20-point gain visible.

**Step 2 is to make the complete 12-card solve fast, which banks the measured +108.** The solve is exact; the only problem is the seconds it takes. Three changes attack that. First, store values for the position after a card is placed and before the next card is drawn, and treat the four columns as interchangeable, so that many positions collapse into one; in the exact solution of a 2048 variant, storing "after-move" positions alone cut the table by **36%** ([Kaneko & Yamashita 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.04580v1)). Second, solve once when 12 cards remain and keep the table for the rest of the game, so the wait happens at most once per game, not once per move (my suggestion; it depends on how the current code caches results). Third, do the work in a background worker while the player is looking at the board, pre-solving for each possible next card. A port of the search core to WebAssembly is an option if plain JavaScript is still too slow, but published JavaScript-versus-WebAssembly speedups are inconsistent blog figures, so the benefit must be measured. Expected gain: **+80 to +110**, the only figure in this plan backed by a project measurement.

**Step 3 is to push the exact solve earlier, to 14 cards and possibly 16.** The last 12 cards already yield about 183 points from exact play over search (75 with the cap plus 108 without), or roughly 15 points per card. Two more exact cards are therefore worth perhaps **+20 to +60** (my extrapolation, unmeasured). The cost grows steeply with each card added, so this step is worth doing only as far as step 2's speedups allow on a phone. The test is cheap to run offline first: compare "exact from 12" with "exact from 14" on the same decks. If the difference is under about 10 points, stop here and spend the effort on step 4.

**Step 4 is to rebuild the value network's inputs and training targets, the largest and least certain prize.** The first 40 cards are played by the network plus shallow search, and that is where most of the remaining gap lives. The project's own results say size is not the limit: a network four times larger scored no better, and fitting exact answers still leaves about 250 points of error. The published remedies are changes to what the network is shown and asked: tell it the columns are interchangeable, give it card counts in a form it can use, have it predict only the points still to come, train a separate version for each phase of the game, and train it on answers from a slower, stronger teacher (the exact solver, and long playouts that end in the exact solver). Expected gain: **+50 to +250** (my estimate; wide because no one has published a result for a game like this).

**Step 5 is search housekeeping, which matters mainly as an enabler.** A shared memory of positions already evaluated, time-based deepening, and safe pruning make the search faster, which pays for steps 2 and 3 and for generating teacher labels. On its own, deeper search is a spent lever: the project measured +27 ± 56 at 60 times the cost.

| Step | What it is | Expected gain (points) | Confidence | Effort |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Same-deck paired testing | 0 directly | High | Days |
| 2 | Fast complete solve from 12 cards | +80 to +110 | High (measured +108 ± 38) | 1-2 weeks |
| 3 | Exact solve from 14-16 cards | +20 to +60 | Medium-low (extrapolated) | 1-2 weeks |
| 4 | New inputs, staged network, teacher labels | +50 to +250 | Low (by analogy) | 3-6 weeks |
| 5 | Faster search (enabler) | 0 to +30 | Medium | 1-2 weeks |

## Why 10,000 is probably beyond even perfect play

Reaching 10,000 needs **+1,160 points, a 13% improvement**, and nothing in the evidence suggests the engine is that far from optimal. Three independent readings point to a ceiling well below it. In the 13-card reduced game, the search is 5.7% below the exact optimum; if that gap held for the full game, perfect play would average about 9,340. The endgame measurements say exact play is worth about 15 points per card over the last 12 cards; if the first 40 cards leaked at the same rate, perfect play would average about 9,550. And in comparable solved games, strong hand-built strategies sit a few percent below optimal: in solitaire Yahtzee, heuristics score 240.67 and 244.87 against an optimum of 254.59, gaps of **5.5% and 3.8%** ([UC Davis summary of Glenn 2006](https://research.engineering.ucdavis.edu/biosport/wp-content/uploads/sites/24/2014/06/Yahtzee-Presentation-03-1.pdf); [Verhoeff](https://www-set.win.tue.nl/~wstomv/misc/yahtzee/trivia.html)). All three put the true optimum somewhere around **9,300 to 9,600**. These are rough extrapolations, not proof, and the full game's long streaks and zero-bust bonus could behave differently from a 13-card game. But a 13% gap would be more than double anything observed.

The 12,650 figure for a player who knows the deck order does not help the case. It measures the value of foresight that no legal player has, and the distance between it and 8,840 is mostly luck that cannot be captured.

The ceiling itself can be pinned down, and that is worth doing before investing in step 4. Solving reduced games of 16, 18 and 20 cards exactly and watching how the 5.7% gap changes with game length gives a trend to extrapolate. This is feasible offline: a stochastic single-player game with **1.15 trillion positions** was solved exactly in about 65 hours on one 16-core desktop with 32 GiB of memory and a few terabytes of disk ([Kaneko & Yamashita 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.04580v1)). If the gap shrinks as the game lengthens, the ceiling is near 9,300 and the plan above captures most of it. If it grows, there is more room than this report assumes, and 9,500 or better becomes a fair target.

## What the network sees matters more than its size

The project's flat result from a larger network matches the best-documented pattern in 2048 research. There, a single network with 1.34 billion weights played better with no lookahead (284,657 against 251,033) but far worse inside a search (326,267) than a network of similar size split into game stages (400,124) ([Jaśkowski 2016](https://arxiv.org/abs/1604.05085)). Gains came from changing what the evaluator could represent, and four such changes transfer to this game.

**Stage-specific networks.** In 2048, sixteen stage-specific evaluators, each initialised from its neighbour, raised searched play from 296,207 to 400,124; without that initialisation, splitting made greedy play worse ([Jaśkowski 2016](https://arxiv.org/abs/1604.05085)). Here the natural stage is cards remaining. The project's late-game network trained on exact labels (-12 ± 27) is a single-stage test of this idea and did not help, so staging should be tried together with the input changes below, not alone.

**Column symmetry and better card-count inputs.** The four columns are interchangeable, yet a plain network has to learn the same pattern up to 24 times. Sorting the columns into a fixed order costs nothing at run time; the alternative is one shared sub-network applied to each column and then pooled. Pooling should include a maximum as well as a sum: on a toy task that depends on the largest element, sum pooling had error 1.90 against 0.14 for max pooling ([Lee et al. 2019](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1810.00825)), and "is there any safe column for a ten" is exactly that kind of question. Strong card-game programs encode each rank's remaining count as a one-of-N pattern, not a raw number ([Zha et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2106.06135)), which lets a small network learn sharp effects such as "no 7s left". Adding inputs that are derivable from existing ones, such as each column's chance of hitting 21 or busting on the next card, also helped in 2048, where redundant features raised searched play from 393,289 to 469,779 ([Jaśkowski 2016](https://arxiv.org/abs/1604.05085)).

**Easier targets and helper outputs.** The network should predict only the points still to come, as a correction on top of a simple calculated baseline; the banked score is known exactly. Learning a correction on top of a decent baseline is an established technique ([Silver et al. 2018](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1812.06298)). Extra outputs for the things the score depends on, such as the chance of finishing with zero busts or of a third bust, are the best-evidenced training aid: removing the equivalent outputs from the Go program KataGo was its largest single loss, equal to needing **1.65 times** more training ([Wu 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1902.10565)). Predicting a spread of outcomes in place of one number has beaten plain regression in several settings, chiefly when targets are noisy ([Farebrother et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.03950)); for exact labels the benefit is smaller.

**A stronger teacher.** Chess engines became far stronger when a fast evaluator was trained on the verdicts of a slow, strong one ([Stockfish 12](https://stockfishchess.org/blog/2020/stockfish-12/)). The teacher here is the exact endgame solver plus long playouts that stop at 12 cards and read off the exact value. In backgammon, such truncated playouts cut the error of a good network by a factor of 6.6, where two-move search cut it only from 0.0120 to 0.00843 ([Tesauro & Galperin 1996](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.05407)). The project's own rollout test (-37 ± 97) was too noisy to settle the matter and used rollouts at play time; the proposal is to use them offline, on shared decks, as training labels. One caution from 2048: training on search results helped only for shallow searches and got worse with heavier ones, for reasons the author could not explain ([Guei 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.11087)). Each round of retraining must therefore be judged by score inside the real search, not by label error.

Before any of this, one quick diagnostic separates two causes of the 250-point error: try to make the current network memorise a few thousand exact labels. If it cannot, the fault is in the training setup or target scale, not in the inputs (my suggestion, not a sourced test).

## Deeper search is spent; faster search still pays

Published results agree with the project's +27 ± 56 for an extra card of depth. In 2048, searched play rose from 511,759 at three moves deep to only 545,833 at five ([Jaśkowski 2016](https://arxiv.org/abs/1604.05085)), and backgammon researchers concluded that with a strong evaluator good play "does not require deep searches" ([Hauk, Buro & Schaeffer](https://papersdb.cs.ualberta.ca/~papersdb/view_publication.php?pub_id=824)). Speed is still worth having because it funds the exact endgame and label generation. The supported techniques are a table of already-searched positions (which cut search time by about 31% on top of pruning in one dice-game study) and pruning at chance nodes using upper and lower bounds on the score ([Veness & Blair 2007](https://cgi.cse.unsw.edu.au/~blair/pubs/2007VenessBlairCIG.pdf)); the latter let backgammon programs search five moves deep where they had managed three ([Hauk, Buro & Schaeffer](https://papersdb.cs.ualberta.ca/~papersdb/view_publication.php?pub_id=824)). This game should benefit more than most from the position table, because many deal orders and column orders reach the same position (my inference).

A full solve of the whole game is not a route to a stronger phone engine. Even if the game proved small enough to solve on a desktop, the 2048 solution needed about 4 TiB of disk for values and still nearly 300 GiB for a moves-only table ([Kaneko & Yamashita 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.04580v1)). That cannot ship in an offline page. Its use is as an offline yardstick and a source of training labels.

## Open-source tools: borrow methods, not frameworks

Searches found **no solver, bot or quantified strategy** for Catch 21, 21 Blitz or similar games; the only strategy material is unquantified tips from an operator's page ([WorldWinner](https://www.playworldwinner.com/games/catch-21/)). Blackjack analysis software solves a different problem and offers nothing beyond card-probability bookkeeping.

The frameworks that handle random events properly are LightZero, whose Stochastic MuZero is tested on 2048 and runs on macOS ([LightZero](https://github.com/opendilab/LightZero)), and DeepMind's mctx ([mctx](https://github.com/google-deepmind/mctx)). Neither is worth adopting. Both require rewriting the game outside JavaScript, and MuZero's main feature is learning the rules, which are already known here. In 2048 it needed about ten times the training data of simpler methods to reach a similar score ([Guei 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.11087)). Money is not the constraint either way: a rented RTX 4090 costs roughly $0.14 to $0.74 an hour ([Vast.ai](https://vast.ai/blog/how-much-does-it-cost-to-rent-a-gpu-in-the-cloud); [RunPod](https://www.runpod.io/gpu-cloud/pricing)), and a network this small trains on a laptop.

The most useful open-source reference is TDL2048+, an MIT-licensed 2048 trainer that implements stage-specific evaluators, restarts from late-game positions and several learning-rate schemes ([TDL2048](https://github.com/moporgic/TDL2048)). Its code is tied to the 2048 board, so it is a source of methods to re-implement in the existing trainer, not code to fork. Its lookup-table evaluator, and the related design used in chess engines ([Stockfish NNUE docs](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/official-stockfish/nnue-pytorch/master/docs/nnue.md)), is a credible alternative to the current network if step 4 calls for more capacity at the same speed.

On the phone, the page should stay as hand-written JavaScript or a small embedded WebAssembly module. WebAssembly with SIMD is supported on about 97% of browsers including iOS Safari 16.4 and later ([caniuse](https://caniuse.com/wasm-simd)), but multi-threaded WebAssembly needs server headers that GitHub Pages still cannot set ([GitHub discussion](https://github.com/orgs/community/discussions/13309)) and that a page opened from a local file cannot have. Ordinary background workers do not need them. iPhones reliably give a page only about 300 MB ([WebKit bug 269777](https://bugs.webkit.org/show_bug.cgi?id=269777)), which caps the endgame table and therefore how early the exact solve can start. A general inference runtime adds megabytes and per-call overhead for no benefit at this network size.

## Conclusion

The project's own negative results are its most useful finding: more depth, more width and more of the same labels are all exhausted, which leaves exactness and representation as the two remaining levers. The first is nearly free, because the +108 is already proven and only needs engineering. The second is a real research bet with no published precedent in this kind of game.

The order of work should be set by a single measurement the project has not yet made: how the gap to optimal changes as reduced games grow from 13 to 20 cards. That number turns the ceiling from an estimate into a fact, and it decides whether the network rebuild is chasing 150 points or 600. Until it says otherwise, the honest target is the low 9,000s, with 9,500 as an excellent outcome and 10,000 as a figure the game itself most likely does not allow.
